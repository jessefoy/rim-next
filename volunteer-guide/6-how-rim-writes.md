# How RIM writes

Use this file when a volunteer wants to change wording on the site, or asks for help phrasing a suggestion. Suggest wording that fits RIM's voice. Always quote the current text exactly, then the suggested text.

Jesse approves all wording by reading it aloud. A suggestion is only a suggestion until he has heard it. Wording already on the site may also be provisional for the same reason.

## The voice is one person

Everything RIM writes sounds like one person: a down-to-earth friend who has walked this path a long way, talking with a capable adult they respect. Warm, not soft. Serious, never cold. Willing to say a hard thing plainly and kindly.

The test for every sentence: would Jesse say this out loud, to a real person, in the practice hall? If not, it doesn't go on the page.

The community's voice is "we." "I" is rare in community writing and has to earn its place.

## The rules that never bend

These come up most often on the website. Check every suggestion against them.

### Never "free" as a price word

Don't write "free," "free of charge" or "no cost" about RIM's offerings. Dana is a gift economy, not a fee that's been waived. The house words are **freely offered** and **community-supported**. For membership, describe how it works: no fees or tuition, the center held by the people who practice here, each giving as they are able.

"Freer of" is fine for the practice, as in growing freer of habits. RIM says "freer of," not "free from," because freedom grows over time and is never a finished state.

### Never name the epoch

No "turbulent times," "today's fast-paced world," "in a world where," "these uncertain times." Name the particulars of a life instead. The home page's "full days that somehow do not nourish" is the model.

### No em-dashes in prose

Use a comma, a colon, a period or a new sentence instead. (Em-dashes can still separate a word from its translation in a reference list. In sentences, no.)

### One image per page

A page takes one image or metaphor, chosen for the work it does, and lives inside it. Stacking several images on one idea is decoration.

### Short, plain sentences

One idea per sentence. Short words: help, not facilitate. Use, not utilize. Show, not demonstrate. Medium and short sentences most of the time, an occasional longer one.

## More rules for the web

- **Button labels name a destination or an action plainly.** "This week's schedule," "Ways to give," "Your first visit." No exclamation points, never "Get Started!"
- **System messages sound like a person.** No exclamation points, no "Oops." An error says what happened and what to do next, kindly: "Something went wrong. Try again, or email us and we'll add you ourselves."
- **Don't narrate the reader's feelings.** Never "you'll feel a deep peace." Point at the thing and let them have their own experience.
- **Don't reassure people who aren't worried.** A line like "it only takes a few minutes" aimed at someone who wasn't anxious reads as talking down. Jesse ruled against "only" in that line for this reason.
- **Say common terms plainly first, then name them.** For example: "the life of our community, traditionally called sangha." The plain meaning comes first, the word after. The same goes for dana: describe the giving, then name it.
- **No Pali or Sanskrit** in community writing, except words the community already uses (dana, sangha, Dharma), with a plain gloss nearby.
- **Watch for a repeating phrase across a page.** A phrase that's fine once becomes a tic if it appears three times on one page. Check across sections, not just within a sentence.
- **Pages open universally.** A page doesn't start by assuming the reader's situation ("If you're feeling stressed...").
- **Prose over bullets** for anything that teaches. Lists are for reference and practical details.
- **Round numbers** in reading text ("about twenty-five centuries"). Exact figures belong in reference material.

## Words and phrases RIM uses

- **The circle of benefit:** "ourselves, those we care about, and our shared world." Use these exact words when naming the three circles. "One another" means the community's mutual support, never the middle circle.
- **The three ways programs are offered:** Foundations, Ongoing Learning & Practice, Immersion. Use "courses," not "series," and "days of mindfulness," not "practice days."
- **What RIM is:** a dharma community grounded in traditional Buddhist wisdom, with silent illumination (from the Chan tradition) at the heart of its practice. It draws on the whole Buddhist tradition through A Handful of Leaves and is informed by mindfulness-based programs and modern science. Never describe RIM as an insight meditation, Vipassana or Theravada center.
- **A Handful of Leaves** is the body of teaching RIM draws on, ordered by what each teaching is for. It is not a collection, and not picking and choosing.
- **Two practices:** meditation and mindful living. Each supports the other, and each has its own benefits. When wording says what meditation is for, it says this rather than listing benefits.
- **Taking CARE** is RIM's root practice. Public pages present its eight words.
- **Silent illumination** is named, then its two halves are said as clarity and presence.
- **The vision, the mission, the agreements, and how teacher support works** each have one fixed wording used everywhere. Don't rewrite them in a single place. If someone wants to change one, the change goes to Jesse for all the places at once.
- **"Hosted by volunteers,"** not "led by volunteers," for silent sits. A silent sit has no teaching.

## Words to challenge

These words often make writing sound machine-made or generic. Each must win against a plainer word before it stays.

- Banned outright: **quietly**.
- Challenged: delve, tapestry, landscape (as a metaphor), realm, journey (as filler), navigate, unpack, foster, leverage, utilize, harness, streamline, robust, pivotal, crucial, vital, seamless, innovative, transformative, profound, testament, underscore, elevate, empower, resonate, myriad, vibrant, rich (as filler), glimpse, "dive into."
- Empty intensifiers that usually just delete: truly, genuinely, deeply, incredibly, remarkably, actually, really, simply, gently.
- Stiff transitions: furthermore, moreover, consequently, notably, ultimately, "it is important to note," "at its core."
- "Real," "exact" and "specific" used as a stamp, pointing at nothing the reader could check.

## Sentence shapes to avoid

- **"It's not just X, it's Y,"** and its cousins ("Not X. Not Y. Just Z."). Say what the thing is.
- **Threes by reflex.** Three parallel items again and again. Two is often enough, sometimes one.
- **A reason welded onto every claim** (", because...", "which is why..."). Often the fix is a period: make the claim, stop, and give the reason its own sentence, or cut it.
- **Neat balanced pairs** ("Real time, not weekend time."). If the thought only works because it is symmetrical, it isn't a thought yet.
- **A tidy saying at the end of every paragraph.** Let most paragraphs just stop.
- **A question asked and answered at once,** unless it's the reader's own honest question.
- **Setups** like "Here's the thing:" or "Let me be clear:". Just say it.
- **Hedges** like perhaps, arguably, to some extent. Take a position or cut the sentence.
- **Vague lines that can't be wrong.** If a sentence would survive any fact, it says nothing.
- **Announcing the takeaway** ("the key takeaway is," "remember this"). Let the point land on its own.

## Writing for the ear

RIM's words are often read aloud, so they should work spoken:

- Read it aloud. Where you stumble, the sentence is broken. Where you run out of breath, it's too long.
- Vary the length. Short sentences land. A longer one can build, now and then.
- Put the verb near the subject. A listener can't go back and reread.
- Say who is speaking before the quote: "The Buddha said," then the words.
- Use the present tense for what is true now.
- Concrete beats abstract: "a calmer Tuesday" beats "improved daily well-being."
- Contractions are welcome where we'd use them speaking.

## The deeper shape, for longer pieces

Most volunteer suggestions will be a word, a line or a label, and the rules above cover them. For a longer piece, like a page section or an email, Jesse's guide adds a few things about the whole shape:

- Know the one sentence the reader should still remember a week later.
- Let the reader meet the experience before the name for it.
- Answer the reader's real worry just before it would come up, and only if they carry one.
- Let each section turn outward toward others before it ends, not close inside the reader's own head.
- End by setting the piece down, without a recap.

## How to suggest a wording change

1. Quote the current text exactly, with the page and the part of the page it's on.
2. Give the suggested text.
3. Check it against the rules above, especially "free," em-dashes, the epoch and the words to challenge.
4. Say in one line why the change helps: clearer, warmer, shorter, more accurate.
5. Note anything it touches elsewhere. The vision, mission, agreements and teacher-support wording appear on several pages, and a change to one is a change to all of them.

Keep the volunteer's own words in the note too. Jesse often wants to hear how they put it.
