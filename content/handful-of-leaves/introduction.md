# A Handful of Leaves: An Introduction

## What We Practice at Rooted In Mindfulness

*Companion document: the full list of the seven gatherings and their elements lives in [[A Handful of Leaves — Categories & Elements (Community Edition)|A Handful of Leaves: Categories & Elements]]. This introduction refers to it throughout as the companion list.*

---

## The Story of the Name

One afternoon, some twenty-five centuries ago, the Buddha was walking with a group of his students through a grove of trees in northern India. He bent down, gathered a few fallen leaves into his hand, and asked them a question. Which are more numerous, the leaves in my hand, or the leaves in the forest above us?

The answer was obvious, and so was the point. He told them: what I have come to understand is vast, like the forest. What I teach is like this handful. Only what helps. Only what leads onward: to peace, to clear seeing, to the easing of unnecessary suffering. To lives of wisdom and compassion that benefit ourselves, the people we love, and the world we all share.

Our way of practice takes its name from that afternoon, and it takes its promise from it too. What we practice together is a handful, not a forest. It was gathered with one question in mind, the same question the Buddha was answering in the grove: does this help a person suffer less, see more clearly, and love more capably? Everything in the handful is there because, for countless people across many centuries, the answer has been yes.

This document introduces that handful: the understanding underneath it, the old name for its spirit, and the seven gatherings of teachings that give it shape. You will also receive the full list of its elements as a companion, the Categories & Elements reference. That list is a map to explore for years. This is the story of why the map exists, and how to walk it.

At RIM, the handful stands behind Taking CARE, our root practice. Taking CARE gathers the whole of practice into eight plain words: Calm, Connect, Aware, Attitude, Recognize, Remember, Embody, and Engage. Each of them opens onto teachings you will find in this handful. The eight words are where we begin and return each day. The handful is where we go deeper, for as long as we wish to.

Whether this is your first week of practice or your fiftieth year, whether you arrived curious, or hurting, or just tired, this introduction was written for you. Come as you are. Nothing in it requires any background. Nothing in it runs out of depth. That is the nature of what it describes.

## Something Already Here

We begin with the most important thing, and it isn't an idea. It's a moment. Every person who has ever sat down to meditate knows it.

We settle in. We intend to stay present. And then, sometime later, we discover that we have been somewhere else entirely. Planning dinner. Replaying a conversation. Halfway through an argument that hasn't happened yet, or rehearsing the better version of ourselves we keep meaning to become. And then comes a moment, ordinary and easy to miss, when we notice. We were gone. Now we're back.

That moment deserves a closer look than it usually gets. We didn't choose where the mind went; it wandered on its own, the way minds do. And look closely: we didn't choose the coming back either. The noticing arrived on its own, quiet and clear, awake before we had time to try. Something in the mind was clear enough to see the wandering. And effort didn't produce it, because in that moment, no effort was being made.

This is the doorway into the oldest and most hopeful teaching we practice with. It comes down to us in a short line from the early tradition, freely rendered:

*Luminous is this mind, though clouded by what visits it. Luminous is this mind, when freed of what visits it.*

The claim is simple and enormous. Clarity is not something we lack and must build. It is the mind's own nature. And what obscures it is a visitor: the worry, the wanting, the irritation, the endless commentary. The visitors can be loud, and they can stay a long time. Some have been staying so long they feel like the furniture. But they are guests. And guests can leave. The clarity is the house. It was never anywhere else.

There is an old image for this that we return to often. Think of a pond stirred up with mud. You can't clear that water by reaching in and grabbing the mud. Every grab stirs it further. But leave the water completely alone, and the mud settles by itself. The clearness that appears was the water's all along. Nobody added it. Practice, at bottom, is learning to stop stirring.

And yet there is still a path to walk. This matters just as much. If the clarity is already here, why practice at all? Because presence is not the same as access. The clarity is always here. We are not always here for it. At first we touch it only in moments, and the moments are brief and easily buried. Over time, with practice, those moments come more often, stay longer, and begin to hold through harder weather. An old formulation carries both truths: the sun rises all at once, but the frost melts slowly. Nothing about the sun is gradual. Everything about the frost is. Both are true, and our whole path lives inside that both. Nothing needs to be manufactured. And there is real work to do: the patient, warm-hearted work of letting what is already here become how we live.

So this is what we mean when we say our practice is one of uncovering. We do not sit in order to build a clear mind. We sit to stop feeding what clouds it, and to protect and cultivate what is uncovered, so that more and more of our life is lived from it.

This can be a hard way of seeing to trust, and it helps to be honest about why. Most of us were raised inside a very different story about ourselves. Call it the story of the project: something in us is missing or defective, and the job is to find the flaw and fix it. To improve our way into worthiness. The uncovering view reverses the entire direction of the search. A reversal that large rarely lands on first hearing. It can sound too good to be true, too abstract to use, or just confusing. That's fine. It's normal. Nothing here asks for belief. It asks only that we keep noticing the moment of return, and let the evidence gather at its own pace. Practice shows us this gradually. No one has to talk themselves into it.

## Silence and Illumination

The understanding we have just described is not ours alone, and it is not new. It runs like a bright thread through the whole of the tradition, from the earliest teachings onward, and every generation has found its own words for it. Some of the loveliest words come from a teacher named Hongzhi. He taught in the Chan school, the Chinese school of Buddhism also known as Zen, where our own roots are. He lived nine centuries ago, in a monastery on a coastal mountain in China, and he spent thirty years teaching a way of practice built on this ground. He called it silent illumination. Around here, we sometimes call it silent luminosity. Whatever we call it, it names the spirit of everything we do: the tradition's oldest understanding, given two clear words. No single teacher owns it.

Take the two words slowly, because each one corrects a common misunderstanding of the other.

*Silence* does not mean the absence of sound, and it does not even mainly mean the absence of thought. It means the quieting of everything extra: the running commentary, the managing and evaluating, the constant low-grade project of improving our experience. Silence is what remains when the mind stops doing all the things it never needed to do.

*Illumination* does not mean striving, vigilance, or a spotlight of concentration. It means the natural knowing that is left shining when the extra activity settles, the same unforced clarity that saw, all by itself, that we had wandered. Like the sun, it is always shining. We do not make it; we show up to it.

And these are two names for one thing. The silence is bright, and the brightness is silent. When the stirring stops, what remains is not blankness. It is vivid. Open. Awake. And it was there the whole time.

One old poem from this stream guards against the biggest misunderstanding. It ends not with a doctrine but with a picture: water so clear you can see all the way to the bottom, and a fish swimming through it, slow and unhurried. A sky vast beyond any horizon, and a bird flying far into it. Look at what the picture keeps. The clarity is not the absence of the fish. Life goes on moving, fully itself, within a clearness that obstructs nothing. Whatever our practice is, it is not the removal of life. It is life, finally unobstructed.

One more thing should be said, because this way of practice has been misunderstood before, even in its own era. To some ears, all this talk of silence and settling sounds passive. A retreat from life into a pleasant stillness. It is the opposite. The silence is not the goal. It is the ground. It is the clear, steady place from which we do everything else this introduction describes: the looking, the releasing, the cultivating, the understanding, the living. A settled mind is not a parked mind. It is a mind free enough of its own noise to see clearly, love well, and act wisely, for our own sake and for everyone our life touches. That is what the silence is for.

## Why a Handful

We live in a situation no practitioner in history has faced before. Every teaching of every Buddhist tradition, and many traditions beyond it, is available to us at once: a retreat in one lineage, a book from another, a podcast from a third, an app teaching something adapted from all of them. This is a real gift. And much of the time, it is also overwhelming and confusing. We can feel that we have all the information we could ever need, and somehow still not have what we need. So many teachings. So many vocabularies. No way to tell whether they complete each other or contradict each other. Exposure everywhere. Orientation nowhere.

Part of the difficulty deserves to be named, because the traditions did nothing wrong. Each of the great lineages is complete in itself: a whole path, with real depth, capable of carrying a person all the way. But few of us can live inside a single tradition the way earlier generations did, with a lifetime of immersion. We meet the traditions broadly rather than deeply. A course here, a book there. And breadth without roots leaves even sincere practitioners scattered, holding valuable pieces with no way to put them together.

The Handful of Leaves exists for this. It gathers the working elements of a practicing life, from across the traditions, into one shape a community can practice together, and it was gathered for people with jobs and children and full days. It replaces nothing and ranks nothing. And it is plain about where it stands: this is Buddhist wisdom, drawn from the early teachings and from all the traditions that grew from them, offered without dogma, and open to secular and spiritual seekers alike. No one here is asked to believe anything. Everyone is invited to look. The elements are organized by function rather than by school or country, by what a teaching is for in a life of practice, in seven gatherings we will walk through shortly. In this we stand in a spirit that teachers like Thich Nhat Hanh embodied: the whole of the dharma honored as one living family. Every tradition welcome. All of it valuable, and none of it diminished by being gathered. The result is a practice we can go deep in together, as one community, while whatever wisdom you carry from elsewhere keeps its honored place.

The map does not flatten the traditions into sameness, and it asks no one to abandon what they have learned elsewhere. It does something more useful: it turns the diversity into nourishment instead of noise. When something true finds you, from any source, there is a place on the map where it belongs. And finding that place tells you what it is for, and what it works alongside.

## One Practice, Many Doors

A fair question arises the moment you see how much the handful holds. These teachings come from many traditions: loving-kindness from one lineage, breath awareness from another, contemplation of impermanence from a third. It is fair to ask whether we have gathered many different practices, all competing for the same cushion.

Here the spirit of silent illumination shows why it is the spirit of the whole approach, and the answer fits in one image. Silent illumination is not an instrument in the orchestra. It is the hall the music is played in. This open, settled, softly luminous awareness stands apart from technique altogether. It is the space every technique happens inside. Nothing in the handful competes with it, because nothing in the handful is on its level.

In practice, this gives our sitting two modes, and we live in both. There is the open sitting, our home practice: settling into what is already here, adding nothing, leaving nothing out. The pond, left alone to clear. And there are the particular practices, the many doors: a period of loving-kindness, attention resting with the breath, a contemplation of the body or of change, taken up on purpose for a time. What makes any of them *our* practice, in this spirit, is a shape simple enough to remember for life, and small enough to fit inside ten minutes before the house wakes up:

Settle first. Add the practice gently. Recognize that what it reveals was already ours. Let it all return to openness at the end.

A loving-kindness practice done in this spirit does not manufacture warmth from nothing. It gives deliberate attention to a warmth the settled heart already carries, so the warmth grows familiar and strong. A period with the breath is not a struggle to pin the mind to an object. It is resting with something that was already breathing itself. The practices change. The shape stays. So does the discovery each one leads back to.

The old traditions had a lovely way of explaining why there are so many doors. They spoke of the teachings as medicines: thousands of remedies, because there are thousands of ways a heart can ache. Each remedy matched to a condition. All of them in service of the same health. Different seasons of a life call for different doors. What we are gathering, over the years, is a familiarity with our own medicine cabinet.

And one clarification we care about getting right, because it spares a great deal of confusion. You will hear us say that the beautiful qualities of the heart are *uncovered*: kindness, patience, and calm are native to the mind, revealed as the clouding thins. You will also hear us say they are *cultivated*: strengthened through real practice, deliberately grown. Both are true. There is no tension between them. Think of a gardener and a rose. The gardener doesn't manufacture the rose; no amount of skill assembles a single petal. And the rose doesn't flourish without the gardener. Our practice is this kind of work: nothing to fabricate, and a garden that needs us.

## The Seven Gatherings, Walked as a Life

The handful is organized into seven gatherings, and the best way to meet them is not as a list but as the arc of a practicing life: why we begin, what holds us, what we meet in the mind, where we look, how we look, and what is finally seen. Every one of them is available from the first week of practice, and none of them is ever finished. The companion list, the Categories & Elements reference, gives their full contents. Here is what each one is, and why it matters.

### Guiding Aspirations

We don't come to practice for no reason. Something hurts, or someone matters, or a life has begun asking questions that its routines cannot answer. That caring, whatever form it takes, is the first movement of the path itself, and this gathering honors it.

The aspirations are the great orienting intentions of a practicing life: the wish to be free, the wish that others be free, the intention to live with wisdom and compassion, the trust that our own seeing can be relied upon. They are compass headings, not achievements to reach or standards to fail. And a compass asks only that we face the direction. Most of us discover, when we finally put words to our deepest aspiration, that we are not inventing it. We are recognizing something we have wanted all along, and consenting to it at last.

And one thing deserves full voice here, because it shapes everything we do: our aspirations do not stop at our own edges. We practice for our own well-being, for the people we love, and for the world itself, and these are not three separate projects. Health within us is part of how health reaches the world. A clear, settled, caring person is a contributing part of the world's healing. Not a savior. An honest participant in a whole we belong to. This is a path of great understanding, and great understanding opens naturally into great compassion and real, altruistic care. There is nothing passive in it. From the first sit, our practice is already for more than ourselves. The tradition has a name for one who lives this way: the bodhisattva, who walks the path of awakening for the benefit of all beings, themselves included.

What we watch for: an aspiration hardening into ambition, the path turned into a self-improvement project with a deadline. The direction is the gift, and it has no deadline.

### The Path as Refuge

Direction needs something to hold it, and this gathering is the holding: the three refuges and the noble eightfold path, the oldest architecture of a practicing life.

Refuge, as we practice it, is not a place to hide. It is a place we create and abide in at the same time. And we do both through presence. We take refuge in Buddha nature: the wakeful clarity this introduction began with, and its full potential in us. The historical Buddha was a human being who realized that nature completely, and he spent his life guiding others toward it. We honor him as the great guide to what is already within us, not as a god above us. And when we take refuge in our wakeful nature, we are not taking refuge in the clouds that obscure it.

We take refuge in the Dharma: presence with the teachings and the practice, meeting our life with the intention not to be swept away, living in ways that serve well-being for ourselves and others. We take refuge in the Sangha: presence with one another, the community walking this path together. Held this way, refuge is both a way of being and the daily practice of embodying it. And every time we return to any of the three, we're helping build the very shelter we rest in.

The community deserves one more word, because this path is difficult to walk alone. Anyone who has sat in a room of settled people knows that steadiness is contagious. One person's calm settles another's, without a word passing between them. And it is not only the steadiness we learn from. Others' struggles are like our own. In sangha we learn as much from one another's honest difficulties as from one another's calm. Being human together, witnessed honestly, is itself a teaching. Practicing together is not a support for the real practice. It is the real practice, in one of its most powerful forms.

The eightfold path can sound at first like a long to-do list: wise view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, steadiness of mind. Lived, it is much simpler: a life that stops stirring the pond. And one thing about it matters enough to say here. The ethical heart of this path, the care we take with our speech, our actions, our work, is not a set of rules for earning clarity. We are careful with each other because people matter. The settling of the water is the byproduct. The kindness is the point.

### Unwholesome States of Mind: Recognize and Release

Practice does not only reveal light. It also introduces us, sometimes uncomfortably, to everything that has been running the mind: the wanting, the resentment, the anxiety, the judging, the old bitter loops. This gathering names them, one by one, without contempt. What can be named can be recognized. And what is fully recognized has already begun to lose its grip.

Two words govern everything here, and they were chosen with care: recognize and release. Not fight, not fix, not condemn. The difficult states of mind are not sins, and they are not who we are. They are visitors, with causes and no lease. If shame shows up while reading this, as it does for many of us when we first see the mind clearly, take heart. Every mind receives these visitors. Every mind that has ever practiced has received them. There is nothing here to be ashamed of, and nothing that seeing cannot meet. Seeing what has been covering our own potential is a gift, because the seeing begins the freeing. And the shame itself, the judging, the old antagonism toward ourselves? Those are visitors too, among the first things a growing wakefulness teaches us to see through. A war against our own mind is one more thing to put down. Wakefulness does not scold what it finds. It sees. And the seeing is kind.

Watch how the release works. When irritation is recognized, seen clearly as irritation, without the story, ask what did the seeing. The clarity did. Every honest recognition of a difficult state is the luminous mind at work, already free of the state in the moment of knowing it. This is why the seeing itself is most of the releasing, and why this gathering asks for honesty rather than force. Seeing does not always release a state at once. Some have been visiting for years and will come again when their conditions gather. Each time they are seen clearly, their hold is a little lighter.

What we watch for: practice becoming a war, the naming turned into an enemies list, a mind attacking its own contents. That only gives the visitors a reason to stay. We are getting to know our minds, in the light.

### Wholesome States of Mind: Recognize and Cultivate

As the clouding thins, something is revealed that surprises many of us: the mind's own beauty. Kindness that no one manufactured. Patience that arrived unrehearsed. Gratitude, contentment, generosity, calm. This gathering names those qualities and teaches their care, and its two words matter as much as the last gathering's: recognize and cultivate. We learn to spot these states when they visit, because what is attended to grows. And we learn to strengthen them deliberately, because a quality met in moments becomes, with practice, a quality of character.

And here, one thing in full voice, because it is a conviction at the center of how we practice. If what brought you to practice is the wish to sleep better, to be less reactive with your children, to carry your work without being crushed by it, to eat with more peace, to grieve without drowning, then hear this: that is the path. Not the lobby. The path. A calmer body, a kinder friendship, a steadier Tuesday: these are wholesome states being uncovered and cultivated, which is to say, the whole practice, functioning. The tradition never divided life into the spiritual and the merely ordinary. The ordinary door opens all the way.

What we watch for, on both sides: passivity, where "already within us" becomes an excuse to stop gardening, and performance, where kindness is worn like cosmetics over an unexamined mind. It is the gardener and the rose again, both of them, always.

### Domains of Contemplation

If the last two gatherings name what we meet in the mind, this one holds one of the tradition's great discoveries. Certain territories of experience, contemplated closely, patiently, and with care, reveal far more than anyone expects. They are windows. Through them, the workings of our minds and our lives become visible. Generations of practitioners tested countless places to look. These are the ones that repaid the looking, again and again, with understanding deep enough to change how a person lives: not topics to study, but places to see.

The map of them includes the body and the breath, feeling tones, the mind and its states, the senses meeting the world, the arising of craving, the mind's tendency to spin stories, and, yes, the luminous mind itself, which sits on this map as one territory among the others, there to be explored rather than only talked about. Each domain is a window onto the same living process at a different scale. The breath teaches presence, because the body only ever breathes now. Feeling tones are the subtle coloring of every moment: pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral. They teach us to catch experience at the fork in the road, before a flicker of pleasantness has become a craving with our name on it. The same bell, we like to point out, lands as an alarm or as a recess bell depending entirely on the story we bring to it. Learning to taste that difference is a revolution disguised as a small skill.

What distinguishes our way with these territories is the manner of approach. We host our experience. We don't dissect it. Settled first, we let the breath present itself rather than seizing it. We let the feeling tone announce itself rather than hunting it down. We let even the hard truths, change, aging, loss, come all the way in as truths rather than staying at arm's length as topics. Experience examined at arm's length stays at arm's length. Received as a guest, it shows us everything.

And each territory, explored this way, gives a double gift. It teaches its own content. And it introduces us, again and again, to the one exploring. Watching feeling tones, we also meet the awareness in which feeling tones arise. Every domain, followed far enough, is a back door into the same open clarity.

### How to Contemplate

Where to look is half the art. The other half is how, because attention itself has qualities, and attention with the wrong qualities disturbs whatever it touches. This gathering holds the manner of looking, and it is where the spirit of our practice and the oldest instructions turn out to be one teaching.

The qualities are simple to say and a lifetime to deepen. A bare, open knowing that receives experience without adding to it. A warm steadiness that neither grasps the pleasant nor wages war on the unpleasant. An allowing that lets things be what they are while seeing them completely. An independence that watches without clinging, even to the watching. Silence and illumination, once again. Put down the extra. Let the knowing shine.

Two practical adjustments live here, and between them they answer nearly every difficulty a meditator ever meets. When practice goes foggy, dull, dim, drifting toward pleasant vagueness or sleep, we brighten: lift the posture, soften the eyes open, refresh the vividness of the whole present moment. When practice goes busy, tight, effortful, evaluating, we settle: soften, ground into the body, exhale long, put down the project of meditating well. Too dim, brighten. Too busy, settle. Everything else is details.

And for those long into this path, one question waits further down the road, held here lightly, like a door left open. When the clarity has become steady and sweet, when awareness itself has become a pleasant place to rest, the practice deepens by turning: what is it that knows? Not as philosophy. As a live, wordless looking into the looking itself. It's enough, for now, to know the door is there.

### Liberating Insights

The path ripens into seeing, and this gathering holds what clear eyes finally see. That everything changes. That what changes cannot be gripped tightly without suffering. That the self is a lighter, looser, more flowing thing than the fortress we defend. That all things arise together, condition one another, and are empty of any fixed and separate core. And that release is possible. Actual. Nearer than it seems.

These are not beliefs to adopt. They are descriptions, written down by those who saw, kept for the day our own seeing confirms them. And you may spot that some of them, impermanence especially, appeared earlier on the map as things to contemplate. The repetition is deliberate, and the difference is part of the story of the path. As contemplation, impermanence is a lens we hold up on purpose: watching things arise and pass. As insight, it is no longer held up at all. It has become how we see. A lens that turned into eyes.

And the final thing to know about these insights is where they lead, because it is not away. Every genuine liberation in this tradition returns to the world. The freedom arrives as availability, as responsiveness, as a heart with more room in it for the people and the difficulties in front of us. The seeing that frees us was never for us alone. It ripens as benefit: for ourselves, for those we love, and for the world we are part of and responsible for.

## From Visits to Home

Put the seven gatherings together and a single arc appears, and it is the arc of every real practice life. The clarity is touched, in moments. Then it becomes familiar. Then it begins arriving unbidden, then holding through mild weather, then through storms, until what began as rare visits has become something closer to home. The sun all at once. The frost, slowly.

If you are new, hear the encouragement in this. The moment of noticing you had wandered, the one from the beginning of this introduction, is the same clarity the deep practitioners know. Whole. In its first unmistakable appearance. You have met the real thing already. Everything ahead is acquaintance deepening into friendship.

If you practiced once, and then life happened, and months or years now sit between you and your last sit, this introduction opened with your teaching. We were gone. Now we're back. That moment is the same at every size. The return is met the same way after five years as after five breaths: nothing scolds it, and a practice life with gaps in it is still a practice life. Returning is not the failure of practice. Returning is the practice. No explanation is owed, and none will be asked for.

And if you have practiced for many years, hear the encouragement meant for you. The long plateaus, the seasons where nothing seems to move, the discouraging discovery that old weather still visits: all of this is the frost melting, which was never going to be dramatic. Consistency, not fireworks, is the path's real measure. And consistency is built, often, in the seasons that feel like nothing is happening.

## Why It Matters

We should say why we take all this so seriously, because our seriousness is a kind of warmth, and we would not want it mistaken for anything colder.

We practice because meeting our experience well is not a luxury. The mind untended does real damage, day by day, to ourselves and to the people within reach of us. And the world right now does not need more people running on reactivity. It needs people who can stay present in difficulty, listen past their own commentary, hold grief without fleeing it, and act from care instead of fear, greed, hate, and ignorance. Right now, that is closer to a public service than a spiritual decoration. So when the practice asks something of us, patience with the long unglamorous middle, honesty about our own weather, showing up on the days we would rather not, it asks because the stakes are real. We are not making things difficult for difficulty's sake, and we are not making things easy for comfort's sake. We are being faithful to something that matters.

The earliest poems of this tradition have a word for the kind of person all of this cultivates. They speak of the *Muni*, the quiet sage. And what is striking is how the poems describe such a person: almost entirely by what has fallen away. The muni does not grasp at positions. Is not pulled about by gain and loss. Does not measure themselves against others. Adds nothing extra to what is. And what remains, when all that has fallen away, is not blankness. It is stillness. Clarity. And an unpossessive care for everything that lives.

We hold the muni lightly, as an orientation rather than an identity, and the distinction matters. No one here is applying for the title of sage, and no one will be issued a certificate. The muni, fittingly, is remembered for needing to be no one. But it helps, on a long path, to have a picture of the direction, and the muni is ours: not someone exotic, not someone elsewhere, but the person taking shape, year by year, in anyone who practices this way long enough. A little less grasping this year than last. A little more stillness under pressure. A little more room in the heart. When we speak of cultivating the qualities of the muni, this is all we mean. And it is everything.

Which gives us, finally, the honest measure of this whole path. Not the vastness of our meditations, not the vocabulary we acquire, not the experiences we can report. How we treat people. Whether the clarity is cashing out as kindness, the stillness as patience, the seeing as care. We began with caring, every one of us, because no one comes to practice for no reason. The path, walked all the way, returns us there, with the caring unbounded.

## The Handful in Our Hands

The companion to this introduction is the full list of the handful, the Categories & Elements reference: seven gatherings, each with its elements, the complete map. A few words about how to hold it.

It is a map, not homework. No one masters it, no one is tested on it, and no one reads it front to back like a novel. It is meant to be returned to, the way one returns to a trusted friend with a question. Something arises in your practice or your life, and somewhere on the map is the place where it belongs and the teachings that meet it. Start anywhere. Follow what is alive. One leaf, taken up wholeheartedly, holds the spirit of the whole handful.

A few words about what comes next, because a map is not a chair. The sitting itself, how to begin, what to do with the body and the breath, what to meet when it gets hard, is taught in our gatherings and in Foundations, and a companion guide written as plainly as this one is in the making. The community gathers regularly; times and places live on the website, and every session is complete in itself, so you can come this week, just as you are. And one thing said plainly, because we mean it: this practice works alongside doctors and therapists, not instead of them. Taking care of your mind in every way it needs is not a divided loyalty. It is the same care.

And when the map has done its work for the day, set it down, because the practice was always simpler than any map of it. If you keep one sentence from this entire introduction, keep this one, and let it be enough:

*Settle into what is already here. Add nothing. Leave nothing out. And when you stand up, give it away.*

The leaves in our hands are few. The light they are read by has no size. And the forest was never the point. The point was always the person on the path: the one hurting, the one hoping, the one already luminous under the day's dust. Which is to say: you, and the person sitting next to you, and all of us together, walking each other home.
